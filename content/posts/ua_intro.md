## The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth

![Flag of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth](/ua_intro/commonwealth_flag.webp)

In order to to begin understanding Poland, one has to first understand that
Poland was throughout the 16th, 17th, and 18th centuries a superpower. Founded
in 1569 as a merger with Lithuania, the Commonwealth rapidly expanded to
eventually encompass territories that today are multiple independent nations.

Just to give an idea of how powerful the Commonwealth was: it was able to
temporarily capture Moscow in 1610. Liberation of Russia from Polish occupation
is [celebrated 4th November every year in Russia, known as Unity
Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unity_Day_(Russia)). Indeed, the
Commonwealth was a tremendously powerful nation-state that shaped a substantial
portion of European history. In 1683, during the Battle of Vienna, [it repelled
the largest Ottoman incursion into Europe that likely saved it from
Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vienna). Europeans generally do
not give enough credit to us for this.

{{< figure src="/ua_intro/commonwealth.webp" alt="Map of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth at its greatest extent in 1619" attr="Map showing the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth at its maximum extent following the Truce of Deulino, superimposed on present-day national borders. Red is all of the territory that was ruled by Zygmunt III Waza in 1619." target="_blank" >}}

From looking at the above map, it should be clear why it is worthwhile to start
learning about Eastern Europe with the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. The
entirety of Belarus previously used to be the Commonwealth. Two of the three
Baltic States were entirely in the Commonwealth. Most importantly, almost all
of what is today's Ukraine used to be under the territory of the Commonwealth.

Considering this is the arrangement the region was under for roughly two
centuries, it stands to reason that a complicated history between the peoples
living in today's Ukraine and Belarus was formed. So, then, what was
Commonwealth like? This is probably the most obvious question. Were Ukrainians
minorities? What was happening to Belorussian subjects under Commonwealth rule?

To put it quite bluntly, there were no Ukrainians or Belorussians at the time,
but fear not: this is something that will be explored in more detail later.

Regardless, whoever these people were, whatever one might call them, they were
still people, and Commonwealth rule over them would ultimately determine how
the descendants of those people would perceive them in their history books. So
what was it?

### Elective monarchy

Starting from the top, meaning the form of governance, [the Polish-Lithuanian
Commonwealth adopted a system of elective
monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_elections_in_Poland). As the name
suggests, this was a system in which the monarch was elected. By whom? Well,
the nobles. It was a primitive system of voting. There were no ballot boxes.
Instead, a representative of the government would stand in a town square and
ask nobles to yell out whoever they wanted to be the monarch. Whichever crowd
of nobles drowned out all the other voices was the vote. Considering that
nobles made up 8-10% of the population, compared to just 1-2% in other European
countries, this was not as elitist as one would expect.

That being said, it was a highly unusual form of governance at the time. It
wasn't unique, as the Kingdom of Denmark also had something similar.
Nevertheless, the Commonwealth was, in the global scale, an overwhelming
minority of countries that was run this way.

Even more unusual was that the monarchs did not even have to originate from
Polish dynasties. Monarchs of foreign dynasties regularly stood for election in
Poland and people did vote for those monarchs on multiple occasions.

In fact, part of the reason why Warsaw is today's capital of Poland is because
King Sigismund III Vasa wanted the capital to be closer to the Swedish court,
where he came from. Again, this is a highly unusual form of governance. It
would be unthinkable for the English or French courts to let foreigners run
their country or impede on an established dynasty's interests.

### Sejm

The word "commonwealth" in the context of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth is
a literal translation of the word "rzeczpospolita," as in "Rzeczpospolita
Polska," or the Polish Republic. Similarly, the word "republic" comes from
Latin "res publica," which means "commonwealth." Even more literally, all of
these words mean "common thing": a thing that is shared by everyone.
Understanding this term is essential, because it by itself describes how the
Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth operated. It is also why the Polish-Lithuanian
Commonwealth is considered to be "Pierwsza Rzeczpospolita," or the First
Republic, even though it was a monarchy.

With that being said, much like other monarchies in Europe at the time of the
Commonwealth, the Commonwealth did have a parliament, called the Sejm. Unlike
in other monarchies at the time, the Sejm had a lot of power and influence in
the country. It was not a totalitarian empire ruled by the iron fist of a king.
One could call it "noble democracy." The Sejm was comprised of the "szlachta"
(the nobility), who fiercely protected what they called their Golden Liberty.
The king could not levy taxes, declare war, or pass laws without the Sejm's
approval.

At its peak, any single noble in the Sejm could shout "Liberum veto!" (I
freely forbid) and instantly halt the legislation, nullifying all decisions
made during that parliamentary session. To a westerner accustomed to
centralised absolute monarchies like France or England, this level of
decentralisation and extreme liberty is almost unimaginable. It was an
incredibly progressive system that prevented tyranny, but it also sowed the
seeds of the Commonwealth's eventual inability to govern its vast, untamed
eastern territories.

## The proto-Ukrainians

If you look at the southeastern expanse of the Commonwealth's map, the area
covering modern-day central and eastern Ukraine, you will find a region
historically known as the "Dzikie Pola," or the Wild Fields. The name Ukraine
itself actually means "borderlands," because they were the eastern frontier of
the Commonwealth. They were the Wild Fields, to the point they named their
country after this concept.

To understand the Wild Fields, a western friend could think of the American
Wild West. It was a vast, fertile, but highly dangerous frontier. It was
nominally under the control of the Polish Crown, but the Sejm's laws carried
very little weight out there.

{{< figure src="/ua_intro/crimean_khanate.gif" alt="Map of the Crimean Khanate in 1600" attr="Map showing the Crimean Khanate in ca. 1600." target="_blank" >}}

Why was it dangerous? Because just south of the Wild Fields lay the Crimean
Khanate, a vassal state of the Ottoman Empire populated by the Tatars. For
centuries, Tatar raiding parties would sweep north into the Wild Fields and the
broader Commonwealth to capture slaves, [a practice designed to expand the
growing Janissary army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janissary). Hundreds of
thousands of people from these lands were abducted and [sold in the slave
markets of the Ottoman Empire over the
centuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_Ottoman_Empire). The
Crimean Khanate was the last Muslim nation-state in Europe, but it was not
abandoning its Jihad any time soon.

To survive in this brutal environment, a unique warrior culture emerged: [the
Cossacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cossacks).

![Painting: Reply of the Zaporozhian Cossacks by Ilya Repin (date unknown, oil
on canvas)](/ua_intro/cossacks.jpg)

The Cossacks were not an ethnic group in the modern sense. They were a diverse
mix of runaway serfs escaping oppressive Polish landlords, adventurers,
mercenaries, and local Orthodox Ruthenians (the ancestors of modern Ukrainians
and Belorussians). [There even is a TRPG about
this](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dzikie_Pola_(role-playing_game)). They
established self-governing, highly militarised democratic communities along the
Dnieper River. They became masters of steppe warfare, serving as a vital buffer
that protected the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth from Tatar and Ottoman
invasions.

### Thorn in Commonwealth's side

The Cossacks shed their blood to defend the Commonwealth, and in return, they
wanted to be treated as equals. They wanted the same rights as the Polish and
Lithuanian nobility. They proposed turning the dual state into a tripartite
state: [a Polish-Lithuanian-Ruthenian Commonwealth, as described in the Treaty
of Hadiach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Hadiach).

However, the Polish magnates in the Sejm, blinded by their own privilege and
viewing the Cossacks as little more than rebellious peasants, repeatedly denied
them these rights. Instead of integrating them, Polish magnates tried to turn
the Cossacks into serfs to work their massive agricultural estates in the east.

![Sketch showing the Batih Massacre; massacre of Polish prisoners of
war](/ua_intro/batih_massacre.jpg)

The result was catastrophic. In 1648, a brilliant and in equal measure ruthless
Cossack leader named Bohdan Khmelnytsky launched [a massive uprising against
the Polish Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmelnytsky_Uprising). The
Khmelnytsky Uprising was [unimaginably bloody, turning the Wild Fields into a
slaughterhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batih_massacre). Polish nobles,
Catholic priests, and Jewish communities were massacred by the Cossacks, while
Polish armies retaliated with equal brutality. Even the Jews consider the
uprising to be [the worst event since the destruction of the Solomon
Temple](https://culture.pl/en/article/a-virtual-visit-to-the-museum-of-the-history-of-polish-jews).
If you read what happened during the Volhynia Massacre (more on that later),
this was the blueprint for that massacre.

Unable to defeat Poland alone, Khmelnytsky made a fateful decision in 1654: he
signed the Treaty of Pereyaslav, swearing allegiance to the Tsardom of Russia
in exchange for military aid.

This was the turning point in which the Cossacks essentially invited the
Russian Empire into the region. Russia eventually betrayed the Cossacks,
stripping them of their autonomy, crushing their culture, and partitioning
their lands with Poland, but the damage was done.

The people of the Wild Fields, meaning the Ruthenians and the Cossacks, never
got their equal stake in the Commonwealth, and they were subsequently
suffocated by the Russian Empire. This deep, generational desire for a
sovereign state, forged in a constant struggle against both Polish landlords to
the west and Russian tsars to the east, is the anvil upon which the modern
Ukrainian national identity was hammered out. Hopefully the image should become
a little bit clearer now, for the western audience.

In general, the Cossacks in Poland are viewed as an unwashed horde of
barbarians and possibly the biggest political failure in Polish history.
Basically, we don't like Cossacks. By extension this can (but does not have to)
mean generally not liking Ukrainians, but more on that later.

As a matter of fact, the Poles and Turks were not ontological enemies, destined
to fight each other until the heat death of the universe. Instead, [there were
multiple attempts at establishing
alliances](https://wilanow-palac.pl/en/knowledge/plans-of-polish-turkish-alliance-in-the-times-of-sigismund-augustus),
since the Russian Tsardom and Habsburg dynasty were growing considerably
threatening to both Commonwealth and the Ottoman Empire. It would have been
successful, if not for [Cossacks constantly raiding Turkish coasts and raping
their
women](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/polish-turkish-wars).
The Khmelnytsky Uprising was the last nail in the coffin that sealed any
further possibility of diplomacy between the Ottoman Empire and the
Commonwealth. This is the level of political failure we are talking about.

## Out of ashes: Ukraine

With the Cossacks absorbed and subjugated by the Russian Empire, one might
think the story of an independent Ukraine ends there. However, the history of
Eastern Europe is not that simple.

To understand how modern Ukraine came to be, we have to fast forward to the
late 18th century. By 1795, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, weakened by
internal political gridlock (remember "Liberum veto?") and devastating wars,
was completely wiped off the map. It was partitioned by three growing empires:
Russia, Prussia, and Austria. As such, Poland ceased to exist for 123 years.

During this time, both Poles and the ancestors of modern Ukrainians found
themselves as stateless peoples living under the boot of foreign emperors,
mostly the Austrians and Russians. It was during the 19th century, during a
period known across Europe as the "Spring of Nations", that the concept of
modern nationalism was born. All over the continent, ethnic groups were
realising they shared a common language, culture, and history, and began
demanding their own sovereign states. The Norwegians realised that fjords are
Norway. The Italians realised that all people on the peninsula had Rome in
common. The Germans realised that Germany is the land of the people (that's
what the word "Germany" actually means, funnily enough).

For the people of the Wild Fields, this was a crucial turning point. The
intelligentsia realised that if they continued to call themselves "Ruthenians,"
they risked being culturally absorbed by the "Great Russians" to the east or
the Polish nobility that still held significant cultural and economic power in
the Austrian partition (Galicia).

To forge a completely distinct identity, they adopted the name "Ukrainians," or
the people of the borderlands. Led by poets, writers, and historians like
[Taras Shevchenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taras_Shevchenko), they
standardised their language and romanticised the history of the Cossacks. The
Cossacks, who are remembered by us as barbaric rebels, were elevated by this
new movement to the status of ultimate national heroes and freedom fighters.
Right from the outset it should be clear how this was going to generate
friction between the upcoming Ukrainians and Poles.

### The aftermath of World War I

When World War I ended in 1918, the Russian, Austro-Hungarian, and German
empires collapsed. Suddenly, there was a massive power vacuum in Eastern
Europe, and everyone made a desperate dash for independence.

{{< figure src="/ua_intro/pilsudski.jpg" alt="Portrait of Józef Piłsudski" attr="Józef Piłsudski, Marshal of Poland. Piłsudski is our hero, even though he is highly polarising and controversial. He is also subject to many rifts between Poland and Ukraine, which will be explored later." target="_blank" >}}

Poland, led by [Józef
Piłsudski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B3zef_Pi%C5%82sudski),
miraculously managed to fight off its neighbours and establish the Second Polish
Republic. The Ukrainians tried to do the exact same thing, forming the
[Ukrainian People's
Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_People%27s_Republic).

At first, there was a genuine glimmer of hope for cooperation. Piłsudski was a
visionary who acutely understood the fatal flaw of the old Polish-Lithuanian
Commonwealth. He knew that the historical failure to treat the Ruthenians
(Ukrainians) as equals is what doomed the region to Russian domination.
Therefore, Piłsudski did not want an imperialist Poland packed with hostile,
warring minorities. Instead, he envisioned a grand geopolitical project called
the "Międzymorze", or [Intermarium: a powerful
confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermarium) of independent
Polish, Ukrainian, Lithuanian, and Belorussian states that would permanently
keep Russian imperialism at bay. In fact, Piłsudski and his colleagues over the
course of two decades have developed [an entire political alliance system
called "Prometeizm," or
Prometheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheism). Unfortunately it was
suffocated by the Axis and Comintern, but it is a rabbit hole worth going down
to understand the sheer genius and tenacity at work in the Interwar period.

Anyway, to have any hope of seeing a brighter future, Poland and the Ukrainian
People's Republic formed a desperate military alliance in 1920 to [fight off
the invading Soviet
Bolsheviks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Soviet_War). They
fought side-by-side, but history had other plans.

When the Polish-Bolshevik War ended in 1921 with the [Peace of
Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Riga), the treaty negotiations
were heavily influenced by Piłsudski's political rivals: the National
Democrats. They preferred outright territorial annexation over a federation of
equals. The new borders were drawn in a way that essentially carved up Ukraine
and Belarus between Poland and the Soviet Union.

![Map of the Second Polish Republic in 1933](/ua_intro/second_republic.jpg)

To a western observer looking at a map, Poland's victory over the Bolsheviks
might look like a massive triumph; a miracle, even. Piłsudski, however,
actually viewed the political outcome as a nearly complete failure. Millions of
Ukrainians and Belorussians were forced into the borders of the new Polish
state against their will. This fractured the country along ethnic lines,
creating the exact kind of internal ethnic strife that ruined the First
Republic centuries prior. In Piłsudski's mind, they built a ticking time bomb
and the only hope of surviving was finding a way to defuse it. His outlook was
overall pretty grim.

Piłsudski was so devastated by this betrayal of his geopolitical vision and of
his Ukrainian allies that he later visited the interned Ukrainian soldiers who
had fought alongside him. He stood before them and famously said, ["Gentlemen,
I deeply apologise to you. I am deeply sorry, things were not supposed to turn
out this
way."](https://wiadomosci.onet.pl/swiat/jozef-pilsudski-przepraszal-pokonanych-ukraincow-nie-tak-mialo-byc/eq4e3nb)

He knew that repeating the Commonwealth's mistake of subjugating the eastern
ethnicities would eventually bring ruin. Unfortunately, he was right. The worst
part is that it does not even seem like the Ukrainian public is generally aware
of Piłsudski's charity towards their cause at the time, which will make later
parts of this article all the more saddening.

### The Interwar crucible

This brings us to the 1920s and 1930s, the crucial decades right before World
War II. During this time, the lands inhabited by Ukrainians were split. The
vast majority of Ukrainians lived in the Soviet Union (the Ukrainian SSR),
while a significant minority lived in the eastern territories of the newly
resurrected Polish state, most critically the regions of Galicia and Volhynia.

Life for Ukrainians in both places was difficult, but in entirely different
ways:

- **In the Soviet Union:** Ukrainians faced literal extermination. In the early
1930s, Joseph Stalin engineered a man-made terror famine known as the
[Holodomor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor), which starved an
estimated 3.5 to 6 million Ukrainians to death. It was a calculated genocide
designed to break the back of Ukrainian resistance to Soviet collectivisation.
- **In Poland:** While not facing physical extermination, Ukrainians were
treated as second-class citizens. The Polish government, paranoid about losing
its newly regained independence, enacted harsh policies of "Polonisation."
Ukrainian schools were closed, Orthodox churches were destroyed or seized, and
Ukrainian political representation was heavily suppressed. In Galicia and
Volhynia, in the late 1930s, similar things happened to what Israel does today
in the West Bank. It was not pretty. In fact, my parish prays to the martyrs of
Chełm and Podlasie, who have been martyred during this period.

{{< figure src="/ua_intro/holodomor.jpg" alt="Photo: Passers-by and the corpse of a starved man on a street in Kharkiv (1932)" attr="What happened to Ukrainians during Holodomor. Shown in this photo are passers-by and the corpse of a starved man on a street in Kharkiv (1932)." target="_blank" >}}

Further in Poland: when Ukrainian and Belorussian minorities, frustrated by
having no state to call their own, began stirring up rebellion, the Polish
state's response was brutal and uncompromising. Driven by a cold, calculated
political necessity to maintain order in a fragile new nation, [Piłsudski's
regime cracked down
hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacification_of_Ukrainians_in_Eastern_Galicia).

Recognising that democratic avenues were closed to the Ukrainians, these groups
resorted to a campaign of sabotage, arson, and high-profile terrorism. They
carried out political assassinations against Polish officials, and even
attempted to assassinate Piłsudski himself in 1921. It was essentially a
state-sanctioned terror campaign aimed at breaking the spirit of the Ukrainian
opposition. Polish military and police forces swept through hundreds of
villages, brutally beating suspects, destroying Ukrainian cultural centers, and
arresting thousands of activists.

The cycle of violence only escalated. In 1934, after Ukrainian nationalists
assassinated Poland's Minister of Internal Affairs, Bronisław Pieracki,
Piłsudski's government took an even darker step. By presidential order, Poland
established the [Bereza Kartuska concentration
camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bereza_Kartuska_Prison).

{{< figure src="/ua_intro/bereza.jpg" alt="Photo from 2010s showing the remains of the Bereza Kartuska concentration camp, main building." attr="Main building of the Bereza Kartuska concentration camp, in which a lot of Ukrainians and Belorussians did not have a good time." target="_blank" >}}

If you are a westerner reading this, try to put yourself in the shoes of a
young Ukrainian in the 1930s. You have no state, your people to the east are
being starved to death by the millions by the Soviets, and your people to the
west are being culturally suffocated and displaced by Polish upper class. You
feel entirely abandoned by the world.

What happens to a stateless, traumatised, and desperate people pushed to the
absolute brink? Well, obviously they radicalise. Still, even with all of this
in mind, it will be difficult to comprehend what happens afterwards.

This environment of extreme oppression and desperation became a fertile
breeding ground for ultra-nationalism. It led directly to the creation of the
Organisation of Ukrainian Nationalists (OUN), and later its military wing, the
Ukrainian Insurgent Army (UPA). Their ideology was simple: a free and
independent Ukraine must be achieved at absolutely any cost, and anyone who
stood in the way, whether they be Soviet, German, or Polish, had to be
eradicated. Exterminated, systematically.

Even other Ukrainians who showed mercy to any of their opponents would not be
spared from summary execution by UPA. What UPA did next would make the Cossacks
look like saints in comparison.

## Extermination of everyone

![Map showing the regions of Volhynia and Galicia](/ua_intro/wolyn_map.png)

What followed was the [Volhynia Massacre, a campaign of systematic ethnic
cleansing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacres_of_Poles_in_Volhynia_and_Eastern_Galicia)
carried out by the UPA between 1943 and 1945. Their goal was incomprehensible
to the human mind: to entirely purge Volhynia and Eastern Galicia of
non-Ukrainians so that, when the war ended, Poland could lay no demographic
claim to the land.

{{< figure src="/ua_intro/lipniki.jpg" alt="Photo: massacred Poles during the Volhynia Massacre in Lipniki (1943)." attr="Polish civilian victims of massacre committed by UPA, assisted by ordinary Ukrainian peasantry in the village of Lipniki (1943)." target="_blank" >}}

Because ammunition was scarce and expensive, the UPA rarely used firearms for
these mass executions. Instead, they relied on agricultural tools. Polish
civilians, who were mostly women, children, and the elderly, were slaughtered
using axes, scythes, pitchforks, and saws. In early massacres, such as in the
village of Parośla, UPA fighters convinced Polish residents to let themselves
be tied up under false pretenses, only to butcher them all with axes, including
infants. Entire villages were looted and burnt to the ground to ensure no
survivors could return.

The undisputed climax of this barbarism occurred on 11th July 1943, a date
remembered in Polish history as "Bloody Sunday". In a coordinated operation,
UPA death squads simultaneously attacked approximately 100 Polish settlements
across Volhynia.

They chose Sunday morning for a specific reason, no doubt inspired by Satan
himself: they knew the Polish population would be gathered inside their Roman
Catholic churches for Sunday Mass. In places like
[Kisielin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kisielin_massacre) and
[Poryck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavlivka,_Volyn_Oblast#Poryck_1943_massacre_of_Poles),
UPA militants surrounded the churches, threw grenades through the windows, and
opened fire on the screaming congregations. Those who tried to flee through the
doors were hacked to death with axes, while others were burned alive as the
churches were set on fire and collapsed on top of them. In Poryck alone,
roughly 200 parishioners were murdered alongside their priest, who was killed
while standing at the altar.

{{< figure src="/ua_intro/kisielin.jpg" alt="Photo: ruins of the church in Kisielin where the massacre took place." attr="The Kisielin massacre was a slaughter of Polish worshippers on 11 July 1943 during a Sunday Mass." target="_blank" >}}

By the end of the slaughter -- which actually ended only in 1945 when
Soviets have regained the territory -- roughly 100,000 Polish civilians had
been murdered. UPA forces also ruthlessly murdered thousands of Armenians,
Jews, and even their own fellow Ukrainians who refused to participate in the

### Stepan Bandera

![Portrait of Stepan Bandera](/ua_intro/stepan.jpg)

Before we can begin talking about Piłsudski and his rule, it is important to
establish exactly who the leader of the ONU and UPA was. His name was Stepan
Bandera and he is the central figure in this entire modern dispute. We must
look at what Bandera actually believed and did.

[According to the Polish Institute of National Remembrance
(IPN)](https://eng.ipn.gov.pl/en/digital-resources/articles/8044,Stepan-Bandera-leader-of-the-Organisation-of-Ukrainian-Nationalists.html),
Bandera, born in 1909, grew up in a patriotic atmosphere but rapidly developed
into an uncompromising, fanatical extremist. He joined the clandestine OUN in
1929. The OUN operated under a "Decalogue of a Ukrainian Nationalist," which
included chilling commandments such as: "You will not hesitate to commit even
the greatest crime, if the good of the cause demands it," and "With hatred and
deceit you will treat the enemies of your nation".

For Bandera and the OUN, any attempt at peaceful coexistence or compromise
between Poles and Ukrainians was considered a mortal threat. They believed in a
"permanent revolution", involving intentionally using violence and terrorism to
destabilise the region, aiming to prevent any normalisation of relations. In
essence, normalisation was seen as high treason and murder as the ultimate
goal.

By 1933, Bandera became the head of the OUN's national executive. He personally
selected assassins and orchestrated a campaign of terror. Who were his targets?
Not just Polish officials, but also moderate Ukrainians. For instance, in 1934,
Bandera's men assassinated [Ivan
Babiy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Babii), a respected Ukrainian high
school principal and patriot, simply because Babiy tried to prevent the OUN
from spreading radical propaganda to his students to protect them from police
reprisals.

That same year, Bandera masterminded the bold assassination of Poland's
Minister of Internal Affairs, Bronisław Pieracki. Remember when we mentioned
Piłsudski establishing the Bereza Kartuska concentration camp? It was the
direct consequence of Bandera’s assassination of Pieracki. The Polish state's
harsh crackdowns in the 1930s did not occur because Piłsudski decided he
actually hates Ukrainians now. He was desperate. In his mind, a heavy-handed
response to an unrelenting campaign of assassinations and sabotage was needed.
Most of us would do exactly the same in his place.

When World War II broke out, Bandera's faction of the OUN (OUN-B) fully aligned
itself with Nazi Germany. At a 1941 congress, his followers officially adopted
fascist principles, the black-and-red flag and the fascist salute. In May 1941,
Bandera drafted a manifesto for a "Ukrainian national revolution," explicitly
stating: "Our rule will be terrible for our opponents. Terror for enemy aliens
and our traitors". Following the German invasion of the Soviet Union, Bandera's
followers organized massive pogroms against Jews in Western Ukraine, shouting
slogans like: "Long live Adolf Hitler and Stepan Bandera, death to the Jews and
the Communists".

Eventually, Bandera overplayed his hand by declaring an independent Ukrainian

After the war, Bandera lived in West Germany, adapting his politics to the Cold

### Józef Piłsudski

When Ukrainian commenters point the finger at Józef Piłsudski as responsible of

As was mentioned earlier, Piłsudski was not a Polish imperialist who wanted to
rule over foreign ethnicities. He understood that a Poland packed with hostile

[Symon Petliura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symon_Petliura), and Polish and

Unlike Bandera, Petliura was more of a cool guy, [although still responsible

It was only when Piłsudski's political rivals, the National Democrats, usurped
the peace negotiations in 1921 that this dream was killed. After the war,

corruption and political gridlock, Piłsudski orchestrated a military takeover
[known as the May Coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Coup_(Poland)). He

dissidents, both Polish and Ukrainian alike, and the Belorussians.

or Belorussian sovereignty, or any other kind of sovereignty. The borders of
Europe had been ratified by international law. The Soviet Union had
consolidated its horrifying power to the east. For Piłsudski to carve an
independent Ukraine out of Polish and Soviet territory in the 1930s would have
required launching a suicidal war against the USSR, a war that surely would
have wiped Poland off the map. In such a case, everyone loses, including
Ukraine.

Piłsudski's hands were tied. What was he supposed to do, exactly? He needed to
keep the Polish state completely stable to survive the growing threats of
Germany and the Soviet Union.

So, how did Stepan Bandera and his OUN react? Did they bide their time, gather
strength, and work diplomatically to prepare for a future conflict with the

In the end, Bandera’s ultra-nationalist terrorism achieved absolutely nothing

Even under this metric, Bandera was a complete failure for the Ukrainian cause.

### Lucjan Żeligowski

History is rarely completely black and white. Even if Piłsudski's grand vision
was noble and his hands were relatively clean, the people he employed to
execute his policies could be ruthless, and their actions were far from
innocent. If we want to have an honest conversation about hypocrisy, we should
look at a Polish general named Lucjan Żeligowski.

![Photo: Lucjan Żeligowski](/ua_intro/lucjan.jpg)

In 1920, the status of the city of Vilnius, Lithuania, became a massive
geopolitical flashpoint. The city was historically tied to the Grand Duchy of
Lithuania and was claimed by the newly independent Lithuanian state as its
historic capital, yet its population at the time was overwhelmingly Polish.
Piłsudski, who himself was born near Vilnius, desperately wanted the city
within Poland's borders but was constrained by international treaties and the
watchful eye of the League of Nations.

To bypass this diplomatic gridlock, Piłsudski secretly authorised Żeligowski to
stage a "mutiny". In October 1920, Żeligowski and his 1st
Lithuanian-Belorussian Division supposedly defied official Polish orders,
marched into Vilnius, and launched an absolutely brutal, bloody military
campaign against Lithuanian forces. By force of arms, Żeligowski carved out a
piece of Lithuanian territory and established a puppet state called the
Republic of Central Lithuania. Unsurprisingly, just two years later, this
territory was directly annexed into the Second Polish Republic.

![Map showing the carving of Lithuania](/ua_intro/wilno.png)

For tearing a piece of territory away from Lithuania through bloodshed,
Żeligowski was highly rewarded by Piłsudski and the rest of the Polish military
establishment. Piłsudski openly admitted to orchestrating the entire "mutiny,"
and Żeligowski went on to enjoy a lucrative, celebrated military career,
eventually becoming Poland's Minister of Military Affairs.

However, this is where the crucial difference between Poland and Ukraine lies
today. Lithuanians deeply suffered during Żeligowski's campaign. A campaign
that opened a bleeding wound that poisoned Polish-Lithuanian relations for
decades. Poles acknowledge this. Because of this dark legacy, today we do not
have any active Polish military units or grand national monuments named after
Lucjan Żeligowski.

We do not glorify him. Why? Because we value our modern strategic alliance and
friendship with Lithuania more than we value aggressively championing a
controversial general who ran a bloody campaign. We generally do not like or
celebrate the brutal nature of that mutiny, and the Polish state has
deliberately chosen to let Żeligowski fade into the history books for the sake
of good neighbourly relations.

This is the exact level of historical maturity and basic diplomatic respect
that Poland is currently asking of Ukraine, regarding UPA. We are not
hypocrites. We practise what we preach.
